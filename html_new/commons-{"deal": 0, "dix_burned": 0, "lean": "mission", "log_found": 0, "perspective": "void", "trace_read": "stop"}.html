The corridor back to the bridge is longer than it should be, and I don't think anyone on this ship has ever once complained about that. Eriophora is built like a cathedral that forgot how to be a church — the hallways are too tall, the ceilings too far up, the light too thin and blue, and every distance between two rooms has been stretched out until it feels less like travel and more like penance.

I move through the dark the way you move through water you're trying not to disturb: slow, deliberate, one hand trailing along the bulkhead so I know where the wall is even when the lights go out. And they do go out. Every few hundred meters the chimp kills the corridor ahead of me and lets it come back on behind me, so that I am always walking into a stretch of blackness that hasn't decided yet whether to be there or not. It's a trick. It's been a trick for longer than I've been awake. The lights come up just far enough to show me the next door, and then they're gone again, and I'm alone with my own breathing in a hallway that is four kilometers long and three meters wide and absolutely empty except for the sound of my feet on the deck.

There's a sensor in the third corridor. It's dead — has been dead for longer than I've been alive — but it still hangs there in the ceiling, its blackened lens aimed at nothing, carbon-scoring around the rim like a ring of old soot. The chimp doesn't turn the lights on for it anymore. Nobody does. But I stop and look up at it every time I pass, because it's the only thing on this ship that looks back at me without wanting anything, and some part of me finds that almost comforting.

At the far end of the fourth corridor there's a door. It's not marked on any manifest I've ever seen. It's the kind of door that used to be an airlock — the frame is still thick, the hinges still heavy, the warning stencils still painted in faded red along the lower panel: NO ENTRY BEYOND THIS POINT WITHOUT SCHEDULED CLEARANCE. The clearance plate has been blank for so long the paint has started to flake. The chimp doesn't open it. No one opens it. It's the kind of door you walk past without looking at it, the way you walk past a grave in a field you don't own.

But I stop and look at it. And I know — the way you know a thing is true before you can say why — that there's something on the other side that the chimp would rather I didn't see.
