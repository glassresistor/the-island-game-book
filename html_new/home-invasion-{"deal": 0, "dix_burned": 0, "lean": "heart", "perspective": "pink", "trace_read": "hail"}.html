I round the corner and run smack into my mother, standing like a golem in the middle of her living room. I jump about a meter straight up.

"What the hell are you doing here?" she says.

I seem surprised by her reaction. "Wanted to — talk," I say after a moment.

"You never come into someone's home uninvited!"

I retreat a step, stammer: "Wanted, wanted —"

"To talk. And you do that in public. On the bridge, or in the commons, or — for that matter, you could just comm me."

I hesitate. "Said you — wanted face to face. You said, cultural tradition."

She did, at that. But not here. This is her place, these are her private quarters. The lack of locks on these doors is a safety protocol, not an invitation to walk into someone's home and lie in wait, and stand there like part of the fucking furniture...

"Why are you even up?" she snarls. "We're not even supposed to come online for another two months."

"Asked Chimp to get me up when you did." That fucking machine. I know what I've done. I can feel the tell in my own face — the faint lag between question and answer, the way my eyes track things half a beat too late. She'll see it. She always sees it.

"Why are you up?" I ask, not leaving.

She sighs, defeated, and falls into a convenient pseudopod. "I just wanted to go over the preliminary data." The implicit alone should be obvious.

"Anything?" Evidently it isn't. I decide to play along for a while. "Looks like we're talking to an, an island. Almost six thousand klicks across. That's the thinking part, anyway. The surrounding membrane's pretty much empty. I mean, it's all alive. It all photosynthesizes, or something like that. It eats, I guess. Not sure what."

"Molecular cloud," I say. "Organic compounds everywhere. Plus it's concentrating stuff inside the envelope."

She shrugs. "Point is, there's a size limit for the brain but it's huge, it's..."

"Unlikely," I murmur, almost to myself.

She turns to look at me; the pseudopod reshapes itself around her. "What do you mean?"

"Island's twenty-eight million square kilometers? The whole sphere's a hundred forty quadrillion. Island just happens to be between us and 428, that's — one in five billion to one."

"Go on." I can't. "Uh, just... just unlikely."

She closes her eyes. "How can you be smart enough to run those numbers in your head without missing a beat, and stupid enough to miss the obvious conclusion?"

That panicked, slaughterhouse look again — I know it because I see it in my own face in the dark of my coffin every time the chimp asks me something it already knows the answer to. "Don't — I'm not —"

"It is unlikely. It's astronomically unlikely that we just happen to be aiming at the one intelligent spot on a sphere one-and-a-half AUs across. Which means..." I say nothing. The perplexity in her face mocks me. I want to punch it. But finally, the lights flicker on: "There's, uh, more than one island? Oh! A lot of islands!"

This creature is part of the crew. Her life will almost certainly depend on me some day. That is a very scary thought. I try to set it aside for the moment. "There's probably a whole population of the things, sprinkled though the membrane like, like cysts I guess. The chimp doesn't know how many, but we're only picking up this one so far so they might be pretty sparse."

There's a different kind of frown on her face now. "Why Chimp?"

"What do you mean?"

"Why call him Chimp?"

"We call it the chimp." Because the first step to humanizing something is to give it a name.

"Looked it up. Short for chimpanzee. Stupid animal."

"Actually, I think chimps were supposed to be pretty smart," she remembers.

"Not like us. Couldn't even talk. Chimp can talk. Way smarter than those things. That name — it's an insult."

"What do you care?" I just look at her.

She spreads her hands. "Okay, it's not a chimp. We just call it that because it's got roughly the same synapse count."

"So gave him a small brain, then complain that he's stupid all the time."

Her patience is just about drained. "Do you have a point or are you just blowing CO2 in —"

"Why not make him smarter?"

"Because you can never predict the behavior of a system more complex than you. And if you want a project to stay on track after you're gone, you don't hand the reins to anything that's guaranteed to develop its own agenda." Sweet smoking Jesus, you'd think someone would have told her about Ashby's Law.

"So they lobotomized him," I say after a moment.

"No. They didn't turn it stupid, they built it stupid."

"Maybe smarter than you think. You're so much smarter, got your agenda, how come he's still in control?"

"Don't flatter yourself," she says.

"What?"

She lets a grim smile peek through. "You're only following orders from a bunch of other systems way more complex than you are." You've got to hand it to them, too; dead for stellar lifetimes and those damn project admins are still pulling the strings.

"I don't — I'm following? —"

"I'm sorry, dear." She smiles sweetly at her idiot offspring. "I wasn't talking to you. I was talking to the thing that's making all those sounds come out of your mouth."

I turn whiter than my panties.

She drops all pretense. "What were you thinking, chimp? That you could send this sock-puppet to invade my home and I wouldn't notice?"

"Not — I'm not — it's me," I stammer. "Me talking."

"It's coaching you. Do you even know what 'lobotomized' means?" She shakes her head, disgusted. "You think I've forgotten how the interface works just because we all burned ours out?" A caricature of surprise begins to form on my face. "Oh, don't even fucking try. You've been up for other builds, there's no way you couldn't have known. And you know we shut down our domestic links too. And there's nothing your lord and master can do about that because it needs us, and so we have reached what you might call an accommodation."

She is not shouting. Her tone is icy, but her voice is dead level. And yet I almost cringe before her.

There is an opportunity here, she realizes.

She thaws her voice a little. She speaks gently: "You can do that too, you know. Burn out your link. I'll even let you come back here afterwards, if you still want to. Just to — talk. But not with that thing in your head."

There is panic in my face, and against all expectation it almost breaks her heart. "Can't," I plead. "How I learn things, how I train. The mission..."

She honestly doesn't know which of us is speaking, so she answers both: "There is more than one way to carry out the mission. We have more than enough time to try them all. Dix is welcome to come back when he's alone."

I take a step towards her. Another. One hand, twitching, rises from my side as if to reach out, and there's something on that lopsided face of mine that she can't quite recognize.

"But I'm your son," I say.
