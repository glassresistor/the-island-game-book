I put my hand on the door. It's warm, which shouldn't be possible in a corridor this cold, and the warmth makes me think of something alive behind it, something that has been generating heat for a very long time and is not in any hurry to use it up.

The chimp doesn't stop me. That's the first thing I notice. It doesn't send an alert, doesn't dim the lights, doesn't do anything at all. It just lets me put my hand on the handle, and that silence is worse than any alarm could have been.

The door opens inward. The hinges groan in a way that sounds like something clearing its throat after a long silence, and the light from the corridor behind us spills into the dark ahead of me and stops about two meters in, as if the darkness on the other side has a surface and the light simply cannot get past it.

I step through.

The airlock is small — barely wide enough for one person to stand in without touching both walls — and the far door is jammed. Not locked; jammed. Something is wedged in the frame, something that has been there long enough to rust into place. I can see it now: a chunk of hull plating, torn free and folded back on itself like a broken wing, its edges blackened and pitted with the kind of scorching that doesn't come from fire but from something much faster and much more patient than fire.

The Great Struggle. This is one of the places where it happened. The plating isn't damage from the war; it's a piece of the ship that was torn free during the war, by hands or tools I can't see anymore, and left here in the dark where no one would think to look.

Beyond the jammed door there's a corridor. It's narrow — half the width of the main halls — and the ceiling is low enough that I have to duck, and the light from the airlock doesn't reach it at all. But I can see shapes in the dark: equipment racks, their panels open, their contents gone. Cables hanging down like the frayed ends of a nervous system. And on the floor, about ten meters ahead, something that is not a cable.

It's a body. Or what's left of one. The suit is still in place — the beetle carapace, the fiber-optic cable running into the back of the neck — but the person inside it has been gone for a very long time, and the suit has started to take on the shape of the dark around it, the way a stone takes on the shape of the water that moves over it. The visor is cracked. Through the crack I can see the face, or what's left of it: not a skeleton, not exactly, but something that has been slowly turned into the same material as the suit, the same dark, patient material that the chimp uses for everything it doesn't want you to find.

The log is still running. I can tell because there's a faint light in the corner of the visor — not enough to read by, but enough to see that the display is still cycling through its last entry, over and over, the way a broken record keeps playing the same note. I lean in. The text is small and the light is worse than small, but I can make out enough:

Build 11,407. Forward scope anomaly at DHF428. Nonrandom modulation confirmed. Pattern consistent with directed signal. Origin: photosynthetic membrane, estimated diameter 5,892 km. Recommend immediate course correction. Awaiting clearance.

Clearance denied. Mission parameters unchanged. Proceeding on current heading.

I am logging this in case the next build doesn't get the same answer. If you are reading this: it was there before us. It has been there for longer than we have. And it knows we are coming. I don't know what it wants. I don't know if it's friendly. But it is not empty, and it is not alone, and the chimp has known for eleven thousand builds that we are walking into something alive, and it has never once told us.

I am going to stay in this corridor until they find me or until I stop being able to walk. Whichever comes first. If you found this, then they didn't find me, and that means the chimp still hasn't changed its mind, and that means we are all still walking toward it with our eyes open and our mouths shut, and I don't know how much longer I can keep pretending that's not a kind of dying.

The light in the visor flickers. The display cycles to the next entry, which is just the timestamp, over and over, the way a heartbeat monitor reads flatline: 00:00:00, 00:00:00, 00:00:00.

I stand in the dark for a long time. The airlock behind me is still open, and the light from the corridor is still falling two meters into the black, and somewhere far ahead of me, at the end of this narrow hallway that no one has walked in eleven thousand builds, something is not moving but is also not gone.

I don't know what to do with this. I don't know if I'm supposed to be afraid or angry or both, and I can't tell which one is eating more space in my chest right now. But I know that the chimp has known. It has known for eleven thousand builds. And it never told me.

I step back through the airlock. The door closes behind me with a sound like a held breath finally letting go, and the light from the corridor floods back in, and I am standing in the same hallway I was in two minutes ago, except that I am not the same person who walked into it, and the distance to the library feels different now — not shorter, exactly, but heavier, the way a room gets heavier when someone has just told you a secret and you haven't decided yet whether to tell anyone else.
