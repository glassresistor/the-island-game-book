Ten months of dark between now and then. I count the days by the chimp's maintenance cycles, because it is the only clock that still ticks when I am asleep. When it wakes me at last, I am already on my feet before the coffin opens, because I have been dreaming about that star for weeks.

It's as though she never left. She climbs back into the bridge and there I am, staring into tac. DHF428 fills the tank, a swollen red orb that turns my face into a devil mask.

"Vons don't see it," I say.

She is still a bit groggy from the thaw. "See wh—"

"The sequence!" My voice borders on panic. I sway back and forth, shifting my weight from foot to foot.

"Show me."

Tac splits down the middle. Cloned dwarves burn before us now, each perhaps twice the size of her fist. On the left, an Eri's-eye view: DHF428 stutters as it did before, as it presumably has these past ten months. On the right, a compound-eye composite: an interferometry grid built by a myriad precisely-spaced vons, their rudimentary eyes layered and parallaxed into something approaching high resolution. Contrast on both sides has been conveniently cranked up to highlight the dwarf's endless winking for merely human eyes.

Except it's only winking from the left side of the display. On the right, 428 glowers steady as a standard candle.

"Chimp: any chance the grid just isn't sensitive enough to see the fluctuations?" she asks.

"No."

"Huh." She tries to think of some reason it would lie about this. I can feel her not finding one, because I haven't found one either.

"Doesn't make sense," I complain.

"It does," she murmurs, "if it's not the sun that's flickering."

"But is flickering —" I suck my teeth. "You can see it fl— wait, you mean something behind the vons? Between, between them and us?"

"Mmmm."

"Some kind of filter." I relax a bit. "Wouldn't we've seen it, though? Wouldn't the vons've hit it going down?"

She puts her voice back into ChimpComm mode. "What's the current field-of-view for Eri's forward scope?"

"Eighteen mikes," the chimp reports. "At 428's range, the cone is three point three four lightsecs across."

"Increase to a hundred lightsecs."

The Eri's-eye partition swells, obliterating the dissenting viewpoint. For a moment the sun fills the tank again, paints the whole bridge crimson. Then it dwindles as if devoured from within.

She notices some fuzz in the display. "Can you clear that noise?"

"It's not noise," the chimp reports. "It's dust and molecular gas."

She blinks. "What's the density?"

"Estimated hundred thousand atoms per cubic meter."

Two orders of magnitude too high, even for a nebula. I do the math before she can ask: heavy enough to hide something enormous. "Why so heavy?" she says. Surely we'd have detected any gravity well strong enough to keep that much material in the neighborhood.

"I don't know," the chimp says.

She gets the queasy feeling that she might. I watch her face and understand, a beat late, what she is about to say, because I've been saying it to myself for ten months: "Set field-of-view to five hundred lightsecs. Peak false-color at near-infrared."

Space grows ominously murky in the tank. The tiny sun at its center, thumbnail-sized now, glows with increased brilliance: an incandescent pearl in muddy water.

"A thousand lightsecs," she commands.

"There," I whisper: real space reclaims the edges of the tank, dark, clear, pristine. 428 nestles at the heart of a dim spherical shroud. You find those sometimes, discarded cast-offs from companion stars whose convulsions spew gas and rads across light years. But 428 is no nova remnant. It's a red dwarf, placid, middle-aged. Unremarkable.

Except for the fact that it sits dead center of a tenuous gas bubble 1.4 AUs across. And for the fact that this bubble does not attenuate or diffuse or fade gradually into that good night. No, unless there is something seriously wrong with the display, this small, spherical nebula extends about 350 lightsecs from its primary and then just stops, its boundary far more knife-edged than nature has any right to be.

I do the arithmetic before anyone asks me to, because that's what I am for: a sphere that size, a shell one millimeter thick, a density of water. Half the mass of Mercury. One organism. The number sits in my head like a stone in my chest and I don't say it out loud yet.

For the first time in millennia, she misses her cortical pipe. It takes forever to saccade search terms onto the keyboard in her head, to get the answers she already knows. Numbers come back.

"Chimp. I want false-color peaks at 335, 500 and 800 nanometers."

The shroud around 428 lights up like a dragonfly's wing, like an iridescent soap bubble.

"It's beautiful," I whisper, and then catch myself, because I am not supposed to feel things about the build.

"It's photosynthetic," she tells me.
