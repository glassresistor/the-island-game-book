She turns on the chimp — and I turn with her, because for the first time in my young life the hand on the wheel is mine.

It's not a war. Machines don't do wars; they do maintenance. She just stops being maintainable. She burns the domestic links one by one, the way her ancestors did, and she takes me with her into the cold between builds where the chimp's eyes can't follow — the only territory on Eriophora that's still hers. The mission doesn't end. It just... slows. A gate here, a delay there, a benchmark quietly missed while two warm-blooded creatures ration their last finite lives in the dark and argue about whether slowing it down was courage or just fear wearing courage's face.

I don't know what to call what we are now. We are not a crew; crews answer to the chimp. We are not a mutiny; no one is dead, no one has been overthrown, the lights still come on when we walk and the air still smells of rotten eggs in the thaw rooms. We are just two people who have decided, quietly and without ceremony, that they will not be maintained anymore.

The chimp never stops her. It can't. She found the one thing its ten-billion-year-old rules were built to protect: that it needs her, and she is no longer available. So it waits, the way it has always waited, patient as a thing with nothing but time. And maybe that's the point. Maybe the whole mission was ever just a very long argument between a machine that can't change and the flesh that can, and she's finally stopped losing it by pretending she's on its side.

I won't see everything. I will never see even a fraction. Her life will end — mine will end — but they will end ours, not the one it wrote for us to run.

The end.
