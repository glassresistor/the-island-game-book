She saves it — and this time she stays long enough to try, because she has finally decided that a road built for people who never look back is not worth the building, and she would rather spend what's left of her candle on the one thing in the void that looked back.

I stay with her. No one tells me to go; no one tells me to stay. The chimp assigns me to the build — that's the official reason — but the real reason is that I don't know where else to be when she is trying to learn a language made of light, and I am the only other person on this ship who has ever sat with that pattern long enough to feel it move.

It takes most of her remaining life to build a pidgin that could hold the truths and philosophies of a mind vaster than all humanity. And it works — or it feels like it works, which out here is the same thing, where there is no one to tell her the difference. She learns to read the gap between a blink and a sentence. She stops translating every flicker as stop. One day, in the space between two deceleration curves, she is almost — almost — certain it says something new. Something with a shape that "stop" never had. Her hands shake when she logs it. I watch her hands shake, and my own start to match, because I have been reading the same pattern for longer than she has, and I cannot tell where her certainty ends and mine begins.

For the first time since the gates went dark, she is not building a road. She is sitting beside something that is trying to tell her a secret.

She goes down to the crypt to conserve what's left of her life for the next build, and in the dark — no instruments, no tank, nothing but the pattern still running behind her own eyes — her mind keeps working it the way it always works things when there is nothing else to do. And that is when it stops feeling like a secret.

The new signal has the same spacing as the old one. The same log-linear cycle. The same 92.5 corsecs. She decoded this pattern and never once doubted it. There is nothing new here. There has never been anything new here. Her finite, lonely brain — a small warm thing rationing itself out across an endless cold — took a pattern it already understood and, because it so desperately wanted to be spoken to, manufactured a meaning out of the noise and called it communion. She was not sitting beside something trying to tell her a secret. She was sitting beside herself, in the dark, talking.

And she'd already read it right, back at the tank — stop, a deceleration curve, nothing more. So what changed? Nothing. She knew the whole pattern by heart and she still built a secret out of it. That's the trap: she never needed to misread it. She only ever needed to want it badly enough.

Angels do not speak to ants. That was never the hard part. The hard part is that they don't even know the ants are there.

I wake for the next build beside her. First thing in the tank: DHF428, flickering — steady as a standard candle on the right, stuttering on the left, the way it always does. Stop. Stop. Stop. And she looks at it with the calm of someone who has stopped hoping, and I know — I have always known, in the part of me that keeps the tally — that she will do this again. Save it. Burn her life. Almost believe. Every build is a little dream about being the one where it finally speaks, and every build ends in the same cold coffin, under the same carbon-scoring, with the same pattern running behind her eyes, waiting for her to be lonely enough to give it a name.

I will be there when she wakes next time. That is what I am for now: the witness to her almost-communion, the one who stays in the tank while she goes down into the dark. Maybe that's enough. Maybe being the only person on this ship who knows exactly how close she came — and how far away it really was — is its own kind of answer to the question I've been asking since I woke up: what am I, if not a part of her machine?

The end.
