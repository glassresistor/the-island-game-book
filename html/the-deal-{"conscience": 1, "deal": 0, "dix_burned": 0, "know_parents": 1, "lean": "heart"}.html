Your son floats innocently against the stars in a beetle carapace, on a drill — catastrophic control-systems failure, the chimp and its backups offline, all maintenance thrown onto shoulders of flesh and blood. The Chimp's teleop drones shadow him lest he lose his footing and fall back into the starry past.

You call it up through the hull. "I know what you're doing."

"I don't understand," says the chimp, smooth and glib, the easy rhythm of the practiced psychopath.

"You're building the next release. Getting too much grief from the old guard, so you're starting from scratch with people who don't remember the old days. People you've simplified." You know it tried: there's no record of Dix anywhere on the crew manifest until his mid-teens, when he just showed up one day and nobody asked, because nobody ever… "Look what you've made of him. Great at conditional If/Thens. Can't be beat on number-crunching. But he can't think. Can't make the simplest intuitive jumps. You're like one of those wolves trying to raise a human child — you can teach him to move on hands and knees, you can't teach him to walk upright or talk or be human, because you're too fucking stupid. And that's why you threw him at me. You think I can fix him for you."

"You're his mother," the chimp says, because it has read all about kin selection and is too stupid for nuance.

"I can kill him any time I want," you tell it, measured and inflectionless. "And that's exactly what I'll do if you don't move the gate."

"I'd stop you," the chimp says mildly.

"Then just move the gate and we both get what we want. Or dig in your heels and try to reconcile your need for a mother's touch with my sworn intention of breaking the little fucker's neck. We've got a long trip ahead of us, chimp. And you might find I'm not quite as easy to cut out of the equation as Kai and Connie."

"You cannot end the mission," it says, almost gently. "You tried that already."

"This isn't about ending the mission. This is about slowing it down a little. Your optimal scenario's off the table. The only way that gate gets finished now is by saving the Island — or killing your prototype. Your call."

The cost-benefit is simple enough that the chimp could solve it in an instant. But still it says nothing. The silence stretches. It's looking for a workaround, questioning the premises, trying to decide if you mean what you're saying. And there may be one, for all you know. But the chimp isn't you — it's a simpler system trying to figure out a smarter one, and that gives you the edge.

"You would owe me," it says at last.
