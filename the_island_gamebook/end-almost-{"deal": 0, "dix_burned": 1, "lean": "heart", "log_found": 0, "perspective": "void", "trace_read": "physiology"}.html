I save it — and this time I stay long enough to try, because I have finally decided that a road built for people who never look back is not worth the building, and I would rather spend what's left of my candle on the one thing in the void that looked back.

It takes most of my remaining life to build a pidgin that could hold the truths and philosophies of a mind vaster than all humanity. And it works — or it feels like it works, which out here is the same thing, where there is no one to tell me the difference. I learn to read the gap between a blink and a sentence. I stop translating every flicker as stop. One day, in the space between two deceleration curves, I am almost — almost — certain it says something new. Something with a shape that "stop" never had. My hands shake when I log it. For the first time since the gates went dark, I am not building a road. I am sitting beside something that is trying to tell me a secret.

I go down to the crypt to conserve what's left of my life for the next build, and in the dark — no instruments, no tank, nothing but the pattern still running behind my own eyes — my mind keeps working it the way it always works things when there is nothing else to do. And that is when it stops feeling like a secret.

The new signal has the same spacing as the old one. The same log-linear cycle. The same 92.5 corsecs. I decoded this pattern and never once doubted it. There is nothing new here. There has never been anything new here. My finite, lonely brain — a small warm thing rationing itself out across an endless cold — took a pattern it already understood and, because it so desperately wanted to be spoken to, manufactured a meaning out of the noise and called it communion. I was not sitting beside something trying to tell me a secret. I was sitting beside myself, in the dark, talking.

And I'd called it a heartbeat, back at the tank — physiology, nothing more. Which was almost right, and that is exactly why it fooled me: I was close enough to the truth to mistake my own loneliness for the last missing piece of it.

Angels do not speak to ants. That was never the hard part. The hard part is that they don't even know the ants are there.

I wake for the next build. First thing in the tank: DHF428, flickering — steady as a standard candle on the right, stuttering on the left, the way it always does. Stop. Stop. Stop. And I look at it with the calm of someone who has stopped hoping, and I know — I have always known, in the part of me that keeps the tally — that I will do this again. Save it. Burn my life. Almost believe. Every build is a little dream about being the one where it finally speaks, and every build ends in the same cold coffin, under the same carbon-scoring, with the same pattern running behind my eyes, waiting for me to be lonely enough to give it a name.

The end.
