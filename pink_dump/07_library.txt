She goes to the library to find a way to talk to it, and I tell myself that's all.



I go with her. It's my decision — no one has told me to stay behind, and no one has told me to come either, but when she walks into the library I find my feet following, because whatever she finds out there, I want to be the one who sees it first. No speaking. No thinking out loud. Just follow.



She starts again from first principles, because that's the only honest way to look at a god she can't yet name. I watch her learn what I can already recite but have never been allowed to 
use
: the alchemy by which photosynthesis transforms light and gas and electrons into living tissue; the physics of the solar wind that blows the bubble taut; the lower metabolic limits for a life-form that filters organics from the ether. She marvels at the speed of this creature's thoughts: almost as fast as Eri flies, orders of magnitude faster than any mammalian nerve impulse. Some kind of organic superconductor perhaps, something that passes chilled electrons almost resistance-free out here in the freezing void.



I acquaint myself with phenotypic plasticity and sloppy fitness alongside her, that fortuitous evolutionary soft-focus that lets species exist in alien environments and express novel traits they never needed at home. Perhaps this is how a lifeform with no natural enemies could acquire teeth and claws and the willingness to use them. The Island's life hinges on its ability to kill us; I have to find something that makes it a threat — or so she tells herself, in the voice she uses when she's trying to convince someone who isn't there.



But all I uncover is a growing suspicion that I am doomed to fail — for violence, I begin to see, is a 
planetary
 phenomenon.



Planets are the abusive parents of evolution. Their very surfaces promote warfare, concentrate resources into dense defensible patches that can be fought over. Gravity forces you to squander energy on vascular systems and skeletal support, stand endless watch against an endless sadistic campaign to squash you flat. Take one wrong step, off a perch too high, and all your pricey architecture shatters in an instant. And even if you beat those odds, cobble together some lumbering armored chassis to withstand the slow crawl onto land — how long before the world draws in some asteroid or comet to crash down from the heavens and reset your clock to zero? Is it any wonder we grew up believing life was a struggle, that zero-sum was God's own law and the future belonged to those who crushed the competition?



The rules are so different out here. Most of space is tranquil: no diel or seasonal cycles, no ice ages or global tropics, no wild pendulum swings between hot and cold, calm and tempestuous. Life's precursors abound: on comets, clinging to asteroids, suffusing nebulae a hundred lightyears across. Molecular clouds glow with organic chemistry and life-giving radiation. Their vast dusty wings grow warm with infrared, filter out the hard stuff, give rise to stellar nurseries that only some stunted refugee from the bottom of a gravity well could ever call lethal.



Darwin's an abstraction here, an irrelevant curiosity. This Island puts the lie to everything we were ever told about the machinery of life. Sun-powered, perfectly adapted, immortal, it won no struggle for survival: where are the predators, the competitors, the parasites? All of life around 428 is one vast continuum, one grand act of symbiosis. Nature here is not red in tooth and claw. Nature, out here, is the helping hand.



Lacking the capacity for violence, the Island has outlasted worlds. Unencumbered by technology, it has out-thought civilizations. It is intelligent beyond our measure, and —



—and it is benign. It must be. She grows more certain of that with each passing hour; I can see it in the way her questions change from 
how could it hurt us
 to 
how does it even work
. How can it even conceive of an enemy?



She thinks of the things she called it, before she knew better. 
Meat balloon. Cyst.
 Looking back, those words verge on blasphemy. She will not use them again. I notice that I have stopped using them too, sometime between the first page and the second, without deciding to.



Besides, there's another word that would fit better, if the chimp has its way: Roadkill. And the longer she looks, the more she fears that that hateful machine is right. If the Island can defend itself, she sure as shit can't see how.



I don't have a fear like that. My fear is smaller and older than hers: that one day the chimp will look at this thing — or at me, or at both of us — and decide that we are the ones in the way.
