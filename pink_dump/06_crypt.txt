She shouts back. No reason not to — I shout it too, right beside her, because I've been waiting ten months for this moment and I'm not going to let the chimp decide it for me. And now she dies again, because what's the point of staying up late? Whether or not this vast entity harbors real intelligence, our echo won't reach it for ten million corsecs — another ten, at the earliest, before we receive any reply. Might as well hit the crypt in the meantime. Shut down all desires and misgivings. Conserve whatever life she has left for moments that matter.



I stay on the bridge. The chimp tells me to sleep; I don't want to, but it's already dimming the lights around my quarters like a hand closing over a lantern. So I lie in my coffin with my eyes open and watch the pattern run behind them, cycle after cycle, each one a little slower than the last, and I think about what it means to be the one who stays awake while everyone else goes down into the dark.



She lingers in her coffin, grateful for small and ancient victories, she will tell me later — no. She won't tell me later. This is something she keeps. The chimp's dead, blackened eye gazes down from the ceiling of her suite; in all these millions of years nobody's scrubbed off the carbon scoring. It's a trophy from the early incendiary days of her Great Struggle. There's still something comforting about that blind, endless stare.



I don't know what that feels like. My coffin has no trophies. Mine is clean, white, and silent, the way a classroom is silent before the test.



She washes the rotten-egg stench from her hair and steps into Eriophora's silent cathedral hallways. Sure enough the enemy waits in the darkness, turns the lights on as she approaches, shuts them off behind her — but it does not break the silence. Not yet. The war has gone cold; she just goes through the motions now, rattling her chains like an old married multiplet resigned to hating each other to the end of time. After all the moves and countermoves, the truth is they need each other.



She treats herself to a gratuitous meal in the starbow commons. Three hours after coming back to life, she calls up the forward feeds. DHF428 blinks at her from the center of the lounge: 
Stop. Stop. Stop.
 Maybe it's pure physiology. Maybe this endless cycle carries no more intelligence than the beating of a heart. But there's a pattern inside the pattern, some flicker in the blink. It makes her brain itch.



I find I'm not afraid of it either, which is the part that frightens me. She wants to know what it wants. I want it to want something back. We are two people on this ship who have looked at the same star for ten months, and neither of us has been able to stop looking.
