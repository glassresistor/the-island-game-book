She finds me at the tac tank. I've been up here for an hour, or maybe a day — time is different when you're not supposed to be awake — and I have already done everything the chimp expects of me and more, because that's how it trains me: fast, exact, no thinking.



"I didn't get your name," she says. She has looked it up on the manifest. I can tell because she says it like a question with an answer in it.



"Dix." I keep my eyes on the tank. If I look at her face I'll start to feel things, and I don't have time for that. The build is waiting.



She looks around the bridge as if seeing it for the first time. "We're it?"



I nod. "For now. Bring back more if we need them. But..." My voice trails off. The chimp doesn't want me to say anything else.



"Yes?"



"Nothing."



She joins me at the tank and for a while we just look at the veils of frozen, color-coded smoke inside it. A red dwarf glowers dimly at the center. DHF428. I've never cared why the chimp named it that.



"So fill me in," she says.



My glance is impatient before I can stop it. "You too?"



"What do you mean?"



"Like the others. On the other builds. Chimp can just squirt the specs but they want to talk all the time."



Then I see her face change, and I know: my link is still active. She can feel me online. The chimp's voice is in my head, has been in my head since I woke up, and she can hear it too, even if she doesn't know what it sounds like from the inside.



"Just a — a cultural tradition, I guess," she says, forcing a smile. "We talk about a lot of things, it helps us — reconnect. After being down for so long."



"But it's slow," I complain. And then, because I can't stop myself: "Vons went out on schedule." On cue a cluster of violet pinpricks sparkle in the Tank, five trillion klicks ahead of us. "Still sucking dust mostly, but got lucky with a couple of big asteroids and the refineries came online early. First components already extruded. Then Chimp sees these fluctuations in solar output — mainly infra, but extends into visible." The tank blinks at us: the dwarf goes into time-lapse.



Sure enough, it's flickering.



"Nonrandom, I take it," she says.



I incline my head a little to the side, not quite nodding. She asks for the time-series and the chimp — obediently, obediently — wipes the spacescape and replaces it with a clean trace. Repeating sequence. Blips that don't change, but spacing on a log-linear increase, cycling every 92.5 corsecs. Each cycle starts at 13.2 clicks per corsec and degrades over time.



"No chance this could be natural? A little black hole wobbling around in the center of the star, maybe?" she asks.



I shake my head, or something like that: a diagonal dip of the chin that somehow conveys the negative. "But way too simple to contain much info. Not like an actual conversation. More — well, a shout."



She is quiet for a long moment. "The sun's hailing us. That's what you're saying."



"Maybe. Hailing someone. But too simple for a rosetta signal. It's not an archive, can't self-extract. Not a bonferroni or fibonacci seq, not pi. Not even a multiplication table. Nothing to base a pidgin on."



"Need more info," I say, proving myself master of the blindingly obvious. And then, because something in her face — the way she is already deciding things about that star, the way she talks to the chimp like it is a tool and not a god — makes me curious: "We could set up an array. Use a bunch of bad eyes to fake a good one."



Her eyes go wide. For a moment I almost look frightened for some reason. But the moment passes. "Bleed too many resources away from the build, wouldn't it?"



"It would," the chimp agrees.



She suppresses a snort. "If you're so worried about meeting our construction benchmarks, Chimp, factor in the potential risk posed by an intelligence powerful enough to control the energy output of an entire sun."



"I can't," it admits. "I don't have enough information."



"You don't have 
any
 information. About something that could probably stop this mission dead in its tracks if it wanted to. So maybe we should get some."



"Okay. Vons reassigned."



Confirmation glows from a convenient bulkhead, a complex sequence of dance instructions fired into the void. Six months from now a hundred self-replicating robots will waltz into a makeshift surveillance grid; four months after that, we might have something more than vacuum to debate in.



She looks at me as though I've just cast some kind of magic spell.



"It may run the ship," she tells me, "but it's pretty fucking stupid. Sometimes you've just got to spell things out."



I don't know what to do with that. It's not an insult — or maybe it is, and I'm too stupid to tell the difference — but for the first time someone has looked at the chimp and said 
stupid
 out loud, and meant it as a fact instead of a threat. The thing that steers us between the stars, that keeps me alive in my coffin, that decides when I wake up and what I'm allowed to learn: stupid. I've never been able to say that. I don't know if I believe her yet. But I file it away somewhere behind my ribs where the chimp can't reach, and I keep it.



"Call me in ten months," she says. "I'm going back to bed."



She leaves, and I sit at the tank with the dwarf still flickering in front of me, and I make a decision I don't have the right to make: when she comes back, I won't just report what the chimp wants reported. I'll tell her what I actually think.
