I move the gate. I moved it exactly as far as the Island told me to, and I tell myself that's all it ever was — a request, from a being so vast and so old that asking is the only verb it knows.



We do not stop. There is no force in the universe that can even slow us down. Past equals present; Eriophora dives through the center of the gate in a nanosecond. The unimaginable mass of her cold black heart snags some distant dimension, drags it screaming to the here and now. The booted portal erupts behind us, blossoms into a great blinding corona, every wavelength lethal to every living thing. Our aft filters clamp down tight.



The scorching wavefront chases us into the darkness as it has a thousand times before. In time, as always, the birth pangs will subside. The wormhole will settle in its collar. And just maybe, we will still be close enough to glimpse some new transcendent monstrosity emerging from that magic doorway.



"Maybe we're missing something," I say.



"We miss almost everything," she tells me.



DHF428 shifts red behind us. Lensing artifacts wink in our rearview; the gate has stabilized and the wormhole's online, blowing light and space and time in an iridescent bubble from its great metal mouth. We'll keep looking over our shoulders right up until we pass the Rayleigh Limit, far past the point it'll do any good. So far, though, nothing's come out.



"Maybe our numbers were wrong," I say. "Maybe we made a mistake."



Our numbers were right. An hour doesn't pass when she doesn't check them again. The Island just had — enemies, I guess. Victims, anyway.



She was right about one thing, though. That fucker was smart. To see us coming, to figure out how to talk to us; to use us as a weapon, to turn a threat to its very existence into a, a...



I guess flyswatter is as good a word as any.



"Maybe there was a war," she mumbles. "Maybe it wanted the real estate. Or maybe it was just some — family squabble."



"Maybe didn't know," I suggest. "Maybe thought those coordinates were empty."



Why would you think that, I wonder. Why would you even care? And then it dawns on her: I don't, not about the Island, anyway. No more than I ever did. I'm not inventing these rosy alternatives for myself.



I am trying to comfort her.



She doesn't need to be coddled, though. She was a fool: she let herself believe in life without conflict, in sentience without sin. For a little while she dwelt in a dream world where life was unselfish and unmanipulative, where every living thing did not struggle to exist at the expense of other life. She deified that which she could not understand, when in the end it was all too easily understood.



But she's better now. And maybe — just maybe — so am I. It's over: another build, another benchmark, another irreplaceable slice of her life that brings our task no closer to completion. It doesn't matter how successful we are. It doesn't matter how well we do our job. Mission accomplished is a meaningless phrase on Eriophora, an ironic oxymoron at best. There may one day be failure, but there is no finish line. We go on forever, crawling across the universe like ants, dragging your goddamned superhighway behind us.



I still have so much to learn.



I wonder if you'll notice the corpse we left behind.




The end.
